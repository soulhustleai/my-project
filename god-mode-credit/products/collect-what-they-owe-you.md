# CHAPTER 1: THE LAW THEY PRAY YOU NEVER READ

## What Is the FDCPA (And Why Should You Care)?

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act — **15 U.S.C. §§ 1692-1692p** — is a federal law passed in 1977 that basically says: "Debt collectors, y'all cannot do whatever you want."

Congress looked at how debt collectors were treating people and said: "Nah. This ain't it."

So they wrote a law that:
- Lists EXACTLY what collectors can and can't do
- Lets YOU sue them when they break the rules
- Makes THEM pay YOUR attorney fees (yes, really)
- Gives you up to $1,000 per lawsuit just for the violation — you don't even need to prove you were harmed

**Translation:** Every time a debt collector breaks this law, they basically handed you a lottery ticket. And you're about to learn how to cash it.

Think of the FDCPA like the rules of basketball. The debt collector is playing the game. But every time they travel, double dribble, or foul you — the ref (the court) gives YOU free throws. And in this game, free throws are worth $1,000 each. They just keep fouling. You just keep shooting.

### Who Does This Apply To?

The FDCPA covers **third-party debt collectors** — meaning:
- Collection agencies (Portfolio Recovery, Midland Credit, Encore Capital, etc.)
- Debt buyers (companies that buy old debt for pennies on the dollar)
- Collection attorneys who regularly collect debts

**Who it does NOT cover:**
- The original creditor collecting their own debt (your bank, hospital, credit card company)
- Business debts (this is for personal, family, and household debts only)

> **Quick test:** Did the letter come from the company you originally owed, or from some random company you've never heard of? If it's a company you've never heard of — that's a third-party collector, and the FDCPA applies. Game on.

## Violation #1 — Calling at Stupid Hours
**Law:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692c(a)(1)

Debt collectors cannot call you **before 8:00 AM or after 9:00 PM** in YOUR local time zone. Not their time zone. Yours.

That 7:15 AM call that woke you up? That's not just rude — it's a federal violation.

**What to do:** Check your call log. Screenshot the timestamp. If they left a voicemail, save it — that voicemail IS the evidence. A timestamped voicemail before 8 AM is what lawyers call a "facial violation," meaning the evidence literally proves itself.

> **Real talk:** One early morning call with a saved voicemail is often enough for an attorney to take your case. That's how powerful this is.

## Violation #2 — Calling Too Much
**Law:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692d + Regulation F (12 C.F.R. § 1006.14)

Under Regulation F (the rule that implements the FDCPA), calling you **more than 7 times in 7 days** about the same debt is presumed to be harassment. Also, once they actually speak with you about a debt, they can't call again for **7 more days** about that same debt.

**What to do:** Count the calls. Your phone's recent call history is your evidence. Screenshot it. If you see 8, 9, 10 calls in a week from the same number? That's money.

## Violation #3 — Threatening to Have You Arrested
**Law:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692e(4)

Let's make this crystal clear: **THERE IS NO DEBTOR'S PRISON IN THE UNITED STATES.**

Consumer debt is a civil matter. You cannot be arrested for owing money on a credit card, medical bill, or personal loan. Period. When a collector tells you "we're going to have you arrested" or "there's a warrant out for your arrest" — that is a **federal violation** of the FDCPA.

They know this. They say it anyway because it works on people who don't know their rights.

But you know now.

**What to do:** If they say this on a call, write down the exact words immediately after hanging up. Date, time, who said it. If you're in a one-party consent state (most states), record the call.

## Violation #4 — Telling Other People About Your Debt
**Law:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692c(b)

A debt collector can only contact third parties (your family, friends, neighbors, coworkers) for one reason: to get your contact information. That's it. And they can only do it **once** per person.

They CANNOT:
- Tell your mama you owe money
- Tell your boss about your debt
- Tell your neighbor, your pastor, your ex, your landlord
- Post about it on social media (yes, this has happened)

If they discussed your debt with ANYONE other than you, your spouse, your attorney, or the original creditor — violation.

**What to do:** Ask the person they contacted exactly what was said. Get it in writing if you can (a text message like "Hey, some company called me about your debt" is gold). Have them willing to make a statement or testify.

## Violation #5 — Failing to Send the Validation Notice
**Law:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692g

Within **5 days** of their first communication with you, a debt collector MUST send you a written notice that includes:
- The amount of the debt
- The name of the creditor
- A statement that you have **30 days to dispute** the debt
- A statement that they'll verify the debt if you dispute it

If they start calling and never send this notice? Violation.

## Violation #6 — Continuing to Collect After You Dispute
**Law:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692g(b)

If you send a written dispute within that 30-day window, the collector MUST **stop all collection activity** until they send you written verification of the debt.

If they keep calling, keep sending letters, or report the debt to the credit bureaus after receiving your dispute and before verifying? That's a violation. And honestly? This is one of the most common violations out there because collectors just... don't care. Until you sue them.

## Violation #7 — Adding Fees That Don't Exist
**Law:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692f(1)

A collector cannot add interest, fees, or charges that aren't authorized by the original agreement or by law. If your original credit card agreement said 18% interest and the collector is charging 24%? Violation. If they added a $50 "processing fee" that's nowhere in your contract? Violation.

## Violation #8 — Lying About Who They Are
**Law:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692e(11) and § 1692e(14)

Every collector must tell you in their first communication that they are a debt collector and that anything you say will be used to collect the debt. This is called the "mini-Miranda" (yes, like the Miranda rights police give you).

If they don't identify themselves as a debt collector? Violation.
If they use a fake name or company name? Violation.
If they pretend to be an attorney or government official? Violation.

## Violation #9 — Fake Court Documents
**Law:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692e(9)

Some collectors send letters designed to look like official court documents or government forms. Red borders, official-looking seals, "FINAL NOTICE" in big scary letters. If it's designed to make you think a court or government agency is involved when it's really just a collection letter — violation.

## Violation #10 — Suing You in the Wrong Place
**Law:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692i

If a debt collector sues you, they can ONLY file the lawsuit in:
- The judicial district where you live, OR
- The judicial district where you signed the original contract

If they filed in some random court across the state because it was more convenient for them? That's a standalone FDCPA violation.

# CHAPTER 3: THE MONEY — WHAT YOU CAN COLLECT

Now let's talk about the part that matters: **what do you actually get when they violate the law?**

Under **15 U.S.C. § 1692k**, you can recover:

## 1. Statutory Damages — Up to $1,000 Per Lawsuit
This is the automatic money. You don't need to prove you were harmed. You just need to prove they broke the law.

**How it works:** The court can award up to $1,000 in statutory damages per lawsuit (not per individual violation). However, if you have violations from different collectors or clearly separate incidents, your attorney may recommend separate actions. The exact strategy depends on your situation — this is where having an attorney matters.

> **GOD MODE TIP:** The $1,000 statutory cap is just the floor. The REAL money is in actual damages (emotional distress, lost wages) which have NO cap, plus the collector pays your attorney fees on top. A well-documented case of harassment can be worth $5,000-$25,000+.

## 2. Actual Damages — UNLIMITED
This is where it gets serious. Actual damages cover:

- **Emotional distress** — Anxiety, lost sleep, depression, fear. Courts have awarded **$50,000+** for severe emotional distress from debt collector harassment. You don't need a therapist's note (though it helps). Your own testimony about how it affected you counts.
- **Lost wages** — If harassment caused you to miss work or lose your job
- **Medical expenses** — If the stress caused health issues
- **Out-of-pocket costs** — Travel to court, missed work to deal with it
- **Reputational harm** — If they told your boss or coworkers

> **Real numbers from real cases:** Settlements of $5,000-$25,000 are common for well-documented violations. Egregious cases have settled for $50,000+. And that's not counting the debt itself being eliminated as part of the settlement.

## 3. Attorney Fees — THEY PAY YOUR LAWYER

This is the beautiful part. Read this twice.

Under **15 U.S.C. § 1692k(a)(3)**, if you win (or settle), the debt collector must pay your attorney's fees AND court costs. This is mandatory — the statute says the court "shall award" these fees.

**What this means for you:**
- You pay NOTHING upfront
- Your attorney works on contingency (they only get paid if you win)
- When you win or settle, the collector pays the attorney's fees SEPARATELY under the statute
- Your statutory damages ($1,000) typically stay with you in full
- For larger actual damage awards, discuss the split with your attorney upfront — most FDCPA attorneys are very fair because the fee-shifting makes the math work for everyone
- Even a $1,000 case is worth taking because the attorney gets paid by the collector

This is why FDCPA attorneys exist. This is why they take "small" cases. The fee-shifting makes EVERY documented violation worth pursuing.

# CHAPTER 4: THE EVIDENCE TOOLKIT — BUILDING YOUR CASE

A case without evidence is just a story. Let's make sure you have proof.

## Your Call Log (The Most Important Document You'll Ever Create)

Start a dedicated notebook, spreadsheet, or note on your phone. Every single time a collector contacts you, write down:

| What to Record | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Exact date and year | Proves when violation occurred |
| Exact time + your time zone | Proves early/late call violations |
| Duration of call | Shows pattern of harassment |
| Caller ID number | Identifies the collector |
| Name they gave | Proves who you spoke with |
| Company name | Identifies the defendant |
| EXACT quotes of what they said | Proves threats, lies, harassment |
| What YOU said | Shows you requested them to stop |
| How it made you feel | Supports emotional distress claim |
| Any witnesses present | Corroborates your account |

**Write it down IMMEDIATELY after the call.** Courts give way more weight to notes made in the moment than memories recalled months later.

## Save Everything

- **Voicemails:** Do NOT delete these. They are self-proving evidence. Transfer to cloud storage.
- **Letters:** Keep the original envelope (postmark proves the date). Scan everything.
- **Text messages:** Screenshot with timestamps visible.
- **Phone records:** Request your call history from your carrier — this independently proves call frequency.
- **Certified mail receipts:** When you send the collector anything (dispute letters, cease and desist), send it certified mail with return receipt. The green card they sign is your proof they got it.

## Recording Calls

Recording a debt collector call can be DEVASTATING evidence. But you need to know the rules:

**One-party consent states (you can record without telling them):** Alabama, Alaska, Arizona, Arkansas, Colorado, DC, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Minnesota, Mississippi, Missouri, Nebraska, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Rhode Island, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Utah, Vermont, Virginia, West Virginia, Wisconsin, Wyoming

**Two-party/all-party consent states (you MUST tell them you're recording):** California, Connecticut, Delaware, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Montana, Nevada, New Hampshire, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Washington

> **IMPORTANT:** Recording laws change. Before recording ANY call, confirm your state's current law. When in doubt, just announce: "I want you to know this call is being recorded." Problem solved.

> **Pro tip:** In two-party consent states, just say at the start of the call: "I want you to know this call is being recorded." If they stay on the line, they've consented. Most will — and then they'll still violate the law because they can't help themselves.

# CHAPTER 5: THE CEASE AND DESIST LETTER — YOUR FIRST WEAPON

This is where you fire the first shot. And it's free.

Under **15 U.S.C. § 1692c(c)**, once you send a written notice telling a collector to stop contacting you, they MUST stop. The only things they can send after that are:
1. A letter confirming they're stopping contact
2. A notice that they plan to take a specific legal action (like filing a lawsuit)

**Anything else they send or any call they make after receiving your cease and desist is a NEW FDCPA violation.** So this letter is actually a trap — a legal, beautiful trap. You tell them to stop. They don't stop. Each additional contact = another violation = more evidence = more money.

## Sample Cease and Desist Letter

```
[Your Full Legal Name]
[Your Address]
[Date]

[Collector's Name]
[Collector's Address]

Re: Account # [reference number from their letters]

To Whom It May Concern:

Pursuant to my rights under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act,
15 U.S.C. § 1692c(c), I hereby demand that you immediately cease
and desist all communication with me regarding the above-referenced
account by any and all means, including but not limited to:
telephone, voicemail, text message, email, social media, and
postal mail.

This letter shall serve as your formal notice. Any further
communication in violation of this demand will be documented and
may result in legal action seeking statutory damages, actual
damages, and attorney fees under 15 U.S.C. § 1692k.

Sincerely,

[Your Signature]
[Your Printed Name]
[Date]
```

## How to Send It

1. Print TWO copies — one to send, one for your records
2. Go to the post office (or use usps.com)
3. Send via **Certified Mail with Return Receipt Requested** (~$6.80)
4. Write the USPS tracking number on your copy
5. When the green receipt card comes back signed, staple it to your copy
6. That green card is your legal PROOF they received it

> **DO NOT** acknowledge that you owe the debt in this letter. Don't say "I know I owe this but..." Just demand they stop contacting you. Period.

# CHAPTER 6: THE DEBT VALIDATION LETTER — YOUR SECOND WEAPON

Different from the cease and desist. This one says: **"Prove you have the right to collect this debt."**

Under **15 U.S.C. § 1692g**, you have **30 days from their first contact** to dispute the debt in writing. When you do, they must:
- Stop ALL collection activity
- Send you written verification of the debt
- Prove they have the right to collect it

Many collectors can't do this. They bought your debt in a bulk portfolio and don't have the original contract, original account statements, or chain of assignment. When they can't verify? They have to stop. Game over.

## Sample Debt Validation Letter

```
[Your Full Legal Name]
[Your Address]
[Date]

[Collector's Name]
[Collector's Address]

Re: Account # [reference number from their letters]

To Whom It May Concern:

I am writing in response to your [letter/phone call] dated
[date of their first contact] regarding the above-referenced
account.

Pursuant to my rights under 15 U.S.C. § 1692g, I hereby dispute
this debt in its entirety and request full validation, including:

1. The name and address of the original creditor
2. The original signed contract or agreement bearing my signature
3. A complete accounting of the balance claimed, including all
   interest, fees, and charges applied, and the legal basis for each
4. Proof that your company is licensed to collect debts in my state
5. Documentation of the chain of assignment from the original
   creditor to your company
6. Verification that the debt is within the applicable statute
   of limitations

Until you provide full validation as required by law, you must
cease all collection activity, including but not limited to:
phone calls, letters, credit bureau reporting, and any legal action.

Sincerely,

[Your Signature]
[Your Printed Name]
[Date]
```

Send this the same way — certified mail, return receipt requested.

> **The play:** Many people send BOTH letters — the validation request first (within 30 days), and if they verify, THEN the cease and desist. This forces them to prove the debt, then shuts down communication. Now they can't call, can't write, and any violation from here is documented ammunition.

> **Past the 30-day window? Don't panic.** You can STILL dispute the debt — you just lose the automatic right to force them to stop collecting while they verify. Here's what to do instead: Skip straight to the Cease and Desist (Chapter 5), file your CFPB complaint (Chapter 7), and consult an attorney about any violations that have ALREADY occurred. The clock on those FDCPA claims hasn't expired — you have one year from each violation. The game isn't over. It just changes slightly.

# CHAPTER 7: THE CFPB COMPLAINT — YOUR THIRD WEAPON

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) at **consumerfinance.gov/complaint** is free, takes 15-30 minutes, and creates an OFFICIAL government record of the collector's behavior.

### Why This Matters for Your Lawsuit

1. It creates a **federal paper trail** — documented before you file suit
2. The collector is **forced to respond within 60 days** — and their response (or refusal to respond) becomes evidence
3. If they lie in their response? That's another potential violation
4. If they ignore it? That shows willful disregard for consumer complaints

### Step-by-Step

1. Go to **consumerfinance.gov/complaint**
2. Create an account
3. Select "Debt collection"
4. Enter the collector's name (exactly as it appears on their letters)
5. Describe the violation — be specific: dates, times, what was said, which FDCPA section was violated
6. Attach documents (call log, letters, screenshots)
7. Submit — you'll get a case number
8. Monitor the portal — the collector must respond within 60 days
9. **Save everything** — print the complaint, their response, and all correspondence

### Also File With:

- **Your state attorney general** — many states have their own debt collection laws that may give you additional rights
- **The FTC** at reportfraud.ftc.gov — helps build the national enforcement database

# CHAPTER 9: THE POWER PLAY — USING VIOLATIONS TO NEGOTIATE YOUR DEBT AWAY

This is advanced. This is where we turn their mistakes into YOUR financial freedom.

### The Strategy

When a debt collector violates the FDCPA, you now hold a **counterclaim** — a legal claim worth money. And that claim changes the entire negotiation.

Think about it from the collector's perspective:
- They're trying to collect a $2,000 debt from you
- But they called you 12 times in 3 days, threatened arrest, and told your neighbor about your debt
- Now YOU have a claim against THEM worth potentially $1,000+ in statutory damages, plus actual damages, plus their attorney fee exposure

Suddenly THEY owe YOU money. The math doesn't work in their favor anymore.

### The Global Settlement Play

A "global settlement" resolves EVERYTHING at once:

1. **Your FDCPA claim** — they pay you damages
2. **The underlying debt** — they agree to reduce or eliminate it entirely
3. **Credit reporting** — they agree to DELETE (not just update) the tradeline from all three credit bureaus
4. **Release** — both sides agree not to pursue further legal action

### How to Execute

**Step 1:** Document violations thoroughly (you already know how)

**Step 2:** Consult your FDCPA attorney (you already know where to find one)

**Step 3:** Your attorney sends a demand letter. Not a threat — a professional statement of facts:

*"We have documented violations of 15 U.S.C. § 1692d and § 1692c by your client. We are prepared to file a complaint in federal district court seeking statutory damages, actual damages, and attorney fees under § 1692k. However, we are open to discussing a global resolution."*

**Step 4:** Negotiate the global settlement. What you're asking for:
- Payment of statutory damages ($1,000)
- Compensation for actual damages (emotional distress, lost wages, etc.)
- **Full elimination of the underlying debt**
- **Complete deletion of all credit bureau reporting related to this debt**
- Written confirmation of all terms

**Step 5:** Get it in writing. Every single term. Have your attorney review before you sign.

### Real-World Outcomes

- Collector violates FDCPA → pays consumer $1,000-$5,000 → waives the entire $3,000 debt → deletes credit reporting
- Consumer went from owing $3,000 to COLLECTING $1,000-$5,000 AND having the debt removed from their credit report
- Net swing: **$4,000-$8,000 in the consumer's favor**

# CHAPTER 10: THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS — DON'T SLEEP ON THIS

## You Have ONE YEAR

Under **15 U.S.C. § 1692k(d)**, you must file your FDCPA lawsuit within **one year** from the date the violation occurred.

Not from when you discovered it. Not from when you found this guide. From when it HAPPENED.

The Supreme Court confirmed this in **Rotkiske v. Klemm (2019)** — the clock starts ticking the moment the violation occurs.

### Key Rules

- Each violation has its OWN one-year clock. Call on January 15? You have until January 15 next year for that specific violation.
- A pattern of ongoing violations extends your window — new violations = new deadlines
- State consumer protection laws may have LONGER statutes (2-4 years in some states). Your attorney can file state claims alongside federal ones.

### Bottom Line

**Do not wait.** If you know violations happened, talk to an attorney NOW. Building a case takes time, and that one-year clock is unforgiving.

# CHAPTER 11: 2025-2026 — WHAT'S NEW AND WHAT'S CHANGED

The law doesn't stand still. Here's what you need to know RIGHT NOW:

## Regulation F — Still in Full Effect
The 7-in-7 call rule, electronic communication rules, and enhanced validation requirements from Regulation F (12 C.F.R. Part 1006) are fully enforceable. These are your tools. Use them.

## The CFPB Has Pulled Back
Under the current administration, the CFPB has reduced enforcement by approximately 50%. They're focused on fraud affecting military families and clear-cut scams.

**But here's what that changes for you: NOTHING.**

Your private right to sue under 15 U.S.C. § 1692k exists completely independently of government enforcement. You do not need the CFPB to take your case for you. You ARE the enforcement mechanism. The FDCPA gave YOU that power.

## States Are Stepping Up

With federal enforcement scaled back, states are filling the gap:

- **New York City** adopted the SHIELD Rule (February 2026) — the strongest municipal debt collection protections in the country
- **California SB 1286** (effective July 2025) — extended protections to small business debts under $500,000
- **Multiple states** have stricter laws than the federal FDCPA — always check your state's consumer protection statutes

## Medical Debt Changes
The big three bureaus (Experian, Equifax, TransUnion) voluntarily stopped reporting medical debts under $500 and those less than one year old. A broader federal ban was attempted but struck down. However, the voluntary bureau policies remain in effect — know your rights.

## Zombie Debt Warning
Under Regulation F, it is now a **federal violation** to sue or threaten to sue on debt that's past your state's statute of limitations ("time-barred debt"). If a collector is contacting you about a very old debt:
- Look up your state's statute of limitations for that type of debt
- **DO NOT make any payment** — even a small payment can restart the clock in some states
- If the debt is time-barred, tell them (in writing) and demand they stop
