# CHAPTER 1: THE CREDIT SYSTEM DECODED

## Your Credit Score Is Just Your Adult GPA — Let's Stop Pretending It's Magic

Let's get something out the way real quick.

Your credit score is not some mystical number handed down from the heavens by three old men in a basement. It's a math formula. That's it. A calculation. And once you understand the formula, you can reverse-engineer the result.

Think of it like this: your credit score is your **GPA for adulting**. In school, your GPA told colleges "can this person handle work?" Your credit score tells lenders "can this person handle money?" Same energy. Different game.

And just like school — once you know what the teacher wants, you can ace the test every single time.

## The Two Scores Nobody Told You About

Here's where it gets spicy. You don't have ONE credit score. You have dozens. But they all fall into two main families:

### FICO Score
- Created by the Fair Isaac Corporation (yes, a real company named "Fair Isaac" — the irony is not lost on us)
- Used by **90% of lenders** for actual lending decisions
- This is the score that MATTERS when you're applying for a mortgage, auto loan, or credit card
- Has multiple versions (FICO 8, FICO 9, FICO 2, FICO 5... it's giving iPhone releases)

### VantageScore
- Created by the three credit bureaus themselves (Experian, Equifax, TransUnion)
- This is what **Credit Karma shows you**
- Most lenders do NOT use this for decisions
- Range is the same (300-850) but the calculation is different

**Why does this matter?** Because you might check Credit Karma, see a 720, walk into a dealership feeling like royalty, and get told your actual FICO is 668. That's not a glitch. That's two different scoring models giving two different grades on the same report card.

> **CROWNED CONSUMER RULE #1:** Credit Karma is for vibes. Your FICO score is for decisions. Know the difference.

## The 5 Factors: The Recipe Behind the Number

Your FICO score is built from exactly five ingredients. Miss one, and the whole dish is off. Here's the breakdown:

### The Credit Score Recipe Card

| Factor | Weight | What It Means | The Analogy |
|--------|--------|---------------|-------------|
| **Payment History** | 35% | Did you pay on time? | Showing up to class. Miss too many days? GPA tanks. |
| **Credit Utilization** | 30% | How much of your available credit are you using? | You got 10 burner phones. Using 3 is cool. Using all 10? Suspicious. |
| **Length of Credit History** | 15% | How long have your accounts been open? | Seniority at a job. Year 1 employee vs. Year 10 employee. Different respect. |
| **Credit Mix** | 10% | Do you have different types of credit? | A balanced diet. All credit cards = all carbs. Mix in a loan, a mortgage, some variety. |
| **New Credit / Inquiries** | 10% | How often are you applying for new credit? | Walking into 15 stores asking for a job in one day. Each one thinks "why is everyone else saying no?" |

### Let's Go Deeper on Each One

**Payment History (35%) — The Big Boss**

This is the single most important factor. Over a THIRD of your score. One late payment — just one — can drop your score 60-110 points depending on where you're starting from. That's not a typo.

The system tracks:
- 30-day lates
- 60-day lates
- 90-day lates
- 120-day lates
- Collections
- Charge-offs
- Bankruptcies

And every level hits harder than the last. A 30-day late is a paper cut. A charge-off is a full surgery. The good news? The impact fades over time. A late payment from 5 years ago barely moves the needle. One from last month? That's a wrecking ball.

**Credit Utilization (30%) — The Silent Killer**

This is the one that catches EVERYONE. Utilization = the percentage of your available credit you're currently using.

Formula: **(Balance ÷ Credit Limit) × 100 = Utilization %**

Example: You got a card with a $1,000 limit and a $300 balance. That's 30% utilization.

Here's the thing most people don't know: **utilization is calculated BOTH per-card AND overall.**

| Utilization Range | Impact |
|-------------------|--------|
| 0% | Slightly negative (shows no activity) |
| 1-9% | OPTIMAL. Chef's kiss. CROWNED behavior. |
| 10-29% | Good. You're fine. |
| 30-49% | Starting to hurt. Lenders get nervous. |
| 50-74% | Significant damage. Red flags. |
| 75-100%+ | Score in the ICU. Emergency. |

The best part? **Utilization has NO memory.** Unlike late payments that haunt you for 7 years, utilization resets every month when your statement closes. Max out your card Monday, pay it off before the statement date, and your score acts like nothing happened. This is why utilization is the fastest lever to pull when you need a quick score boost.

**Length of Credit History (15%)**

This one is simple but painful: the longer your accounts have been open, the better. That's why closing your oldest credit card is almost NEVER a good idea — even if you don't use it. That card is aging like fine wine. Let it breathe.

**Credit Mix (10%)**

Lenders want to see you can handle different types of credit:
- **Revolving credit** (credit cards, lines of credit)
- **Installment loans** (auto loans, personal loans, student loans)
- **Mortgage**

You don't need all three. But having only one type is like only eating one food group. You survive, but you're not thriving.

**New Credit (10%)**

Every time you apply for credit and the lender does a **hard pull**, it shows up as an inquiry. Too many inquiries in a short period = desperation in the algorithm's eyes.

But here's the exception: **rate shopping.** If you're shopping for a mortgage or auto loan, multiple inquiries within a 14-45 day window (depending on the FICO version) count as ONE inquiry. The system knows you're comparing rates, not collecting cards.

## FICO 8 vs FICO 9 vs VantageScore 3.0 — The Differences That Actually Matter

| Feature | FICO 8 | FICO 9 | VantageScore 3.0 |
|---------|--------|--------|-------------------|
| **Most commonly used?** | YES — this is the standard | Growing adoption | Credit Karma / free apps |
| **Paid collections** | Still counts against you | Ignores paid collections | Ignores paid collections |
| **Medical debt** | Treated like any other collection | Less impact | Less impact |
| **Rent payments** | Not factored | Can help if reported | Can help if reported |
| **Authorized user impact** | Full impact (good AND bad) | Full impact | Less weight |
| **Score range** | 300-850 | 300-850 | 300-850 |

**Why FICO 8 matters most:** The majority of credit card issuers, auto lenders, and personal loan companies pull FICO 8. Mortgages actually use even OLDER versions (FICO 2, 4, and 5). So yes, the mortgage industry is literally judging your financial future on technology from 2004. Make it make sense.

## Myths BUSTED — Stop Believing This Nonsense

**MYTH: "Checking your own credit score hurts it."**
FACT: Checking your own score is a SOFT inquiry. It has zero impact. None. Nada. Check it every single day if you want. The only thing that hurts is a HARD inquiry — when a LENDER pulls your credit because you applied for something.

**MYTH: "You need to carry a balance to build credit."**
FACT: This might be the most expensive myth in credit history. You do NOT need to carry a balance. You do NOT need to pay interest. Use the card, pay it off, repeat. Interest is not a credit-building strategy — it's a bank's revenue stream. Don't fund their yacht.

**MYTH: "Closing old cards you don't use helps your credit."**
FACT: The opposite. Closing old cards reduces your total available credit (hurting utilization) AND removes that account's age from your history calculation eventually. Keep it open. Put a Netflix subscription on it. Set it to autopay. Forget about it.

**MYTH: "All debt is bad debt."**
FACT: A well-managed installment loan or credit card actually HELPS your score. The system rewards you for proving you can borrow responsibly. The key word is "managed." A $5,000 credit card with a $200 balance paid on time? That's a score booster, not a burden.

**MYTH: "Once you pay off a collection, it disappears."**
FACT: Under FICO 8 (the most common model), a paid collection STILL shows on your report and STILL impacts your score. It just says "paid" instead of "unpaid." Under FICO 9, paid collections are ignored — but most lenders aren't using FICO 9 yet. This is why dispute strategy matters more than just paying everything (more on this in Chapter 3).

> **CROWNED CONSUMER RULE #2:** Your score is a tool, not your identity. A 520 today can be a 740 in 12 months with the right strategy. Don't let a number define you — let it motivate you.

# CHAPTER 2: YOUR CREDIT REPORT AUTOPSY

## Time to Cut This Thing Open and See What's Really Going On

If your credit score is the grade, your credit report is the report card. And right now, we're going to go through it line by line like a detective reviewing evidence — because that's exactly what this is.

Errors on credit reports are not rare. They're not edge cases. The FTC found that **1 in 5 consumers** has an error on at least one credit report. One in five. That means if you're in a room with four of your friends, statistically one of you has wrong information on your report RIGHT NOW affecting your score, your interest rates, and your financial life.

So let's find out if it's you.

## Step 1: Pull Your Reports (It's Free — Don't Pay for This)

Go to **AnnualCreditReport.com** — this is the ONLY official site authorized by federal law to give you your free credit reports. Not freecreditreport.com. Not creditkarma (that gives you VantageScore reports, not your actual bureau reports). Not some random site you found on TikTok.

**Key update:** Your free reports used to be available once per year. Since the pandemic, all three bureaus now let you pull your reports **weekly** for free. There is zero excuse not to know what's on your report.

You'll get three separate reports from:
1. **Equifax**
2. **Experian**
3. **TransUnion**

Why three? Because each bureau is a separate, private company. They don't share data with each other. A creditor might report to one, two, or all three — and they might report slightly different information to each one. This is actually USEFUL for dispute strategy (more on that in Chapter 3).

## Step 2: Understanding the 4 Sections of Your Report

Every credit report — regardless of which bureau — has four main sections. Let's break each one down:

### SECTION 1: Personal Information

This includes:
- Full name (and any variations/misspellings)
- Current and previous addresses
- Social Security Number (partial)
- Date of birth
- Current and previous employers

**What to look for:**
- Wrong name spellings (could indicate a mixed file — someone else's info on your report)
- Addresses you've never lived at (identity theft red flag)
- Employers you've never worked for
- Wrong date of birth

> Personal info errors don't directly affect your score, but they CAN indicate that someone else's accounts are mixed into your file. And THAT definitely affects your score.

### SECTION 2: Account Information (Trade Lines)

This is the meat and potatoes. Every credit account you have (or had) shows up here. For each account, you'll see:

| Field | What It Means | Why It Matters |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| **Creditor Name** | Who you owe | Make sure you recognize every single one |
| **Account Number** | Usually partial | Used for disputes — write it down |
| **Account Type** | Revolving, installment, mortgage, etc. | Affects credit mix factor |
| **Date Opened** | When the account started | Affects length of history |
| **Credit Limit / Original Amount** | Max credit or loan amount | Used to calculate utilization |
| **Current Balance** | What you owe right now | Updates monthly (usually on statement date) |
| **Payment Status** | Current, 30 days late, 60, 90, etc. | THIS is what makes or breaks your score |
| **Date of Last Activity** | Last time something happened on this account | Important for statute of limitations |
| **Date of First Delinquency (DOFD)** | The FIRST time you went late and never caught up | THE most important date. More on this below. |
| **Responsibility** | Individual, joint, authorized user | Determines if it's YOUR obligation |

**Line-by-line, ask yourself:**
1. Do I recognize this account?
2. Is the balance correct?
3. Is the payment history accurate? (Every late payment marker should be verified)
4. Is the credit limit right? (A wrong limit = wrong utilization = wrong score)
5. Is the date opened correct?

### SECTION 3: Inquiries

Two types:

**Hard Inquiries** — You applied for credit. These show up and can impact your score for 12 months (they stay on the report for 2 years but only affect scoring for 1 year).

**Soft Inquiries** — Background checks, pre-approvals, you checking your own credit. Only YOU can see these. They don't affect anything.

**What to look for:** Hard inquiries you don't recognize. This could mean someone applied for credit in your name (identity theft) or a lender pulled your credit without permissible purpose (an FCRA violation — and yes, you can dispute that).

### SECTION 4: Public Records

This used to include tax liens and civil judgments, but since 2017-2018, the bureaus removed most of those. Now this section mainly shows:
- **Bankruptcies** (Chapter 7 stays 10 years, Chapter 13 stays 7 years)

If you see tax liens or civil judgments still on your report, those may be removable since the bureaus officially stopped reporting them.

## The DOFD: The Most Important Date You've Never Heard Of

Let's talk about the **Date of First Delinquency (DOFD)** because this one date controls EVERYTHING about how long negative information stays on your report.

Here's how it works:

The DOFD is the date you FIRST went late on an account and **never brought it current again**. Not the last time you were late. Not the date it went to collections. Not the date the collector bought the debt. The FIRST date you fell behind and stayed behind.

**Why does this matter?** Because the **7-year clock** starts from the DOFD.

### The 7-Year Rule Explained

Under the FCRA, most negative information must be removed from your report 7 years from the DOFD. Not 7 years from:
- The last payment you made (**wrong**)
- The date a collector contacted you (**wrong**)
- The date the account was sold to a new collector (**wrong**)
- The last activity date (**WRONG**)

From. The. DOFD. Period.

```
TIMELINE EXAMPLE:

Jan 2020: You miss a payment on a credit card (DOFD = Jan 2020)
Mar 2020: Account goes to collections
Jun 2021: Collection agency sells debt to a NEW collector
Sep 2023: New collector calls you and you make a $50 payment

WHEN DOES IT FALL OFF YOUR REPORT?
→ Jan 2027 (7 years from DOFD)
NOT Sep 2030. NOT Jun 2028. January 2027.
```

> **CROWNED CONSUMER RULE #3:** NEVER let a collector trick you into making a payment that "resets the clock." Under FCRA, the 7-year period starts from DOFD and CANNOT be legally restarted. However, making a payment CAN restart the statute of limitations for LAWSUITS in some states (different from credit reporting). Know the difference.

## How to Spot Re-Aging (It's Illegal — And More Common Than You Think)

**Re-aging** is when a collector changes the DOFD on your report to make a debt appear newer than it actually is. This is a violation of the FCRA and it happens ALL THE TIME.

Here's how to catch it:

1. Pull reports from ALL THREE bureaus
2. Find the same collection account on each one
3. Compare the DOFD across all three reports

If the DOFD is different on different bureaus, someone changed it. And that's a dispute you can win.

### Why Online Disputes Are the Weakest Play

When you dispute online through Equifax.com, Experian.com, or TransUnion.com, here's what actually happens behind the scenes:

1. You type out your detailed explanation of why the account is wrong
2. A bureau employee (or algorithm) reads your paragraph and converts it into a **2-3 digit e-OSCAR code**
3. That code gets sent to the creditor/collector
4. The creditor/collector sees the code, checks if the account exists in THEIR system, and clicks "verified"
5. You get a letter saying "we investigated and the information is accurate"

That's the "investigation." Your detailed explanation got compressed into a code like "not mine" or "wrong balance." The nuance? Gone. The legal citations? Ignored. Your frustration? Maximum.

**e-OSCAR** (Online Solution for Complete and Accurate Reporting) is the system the bureaus use to communicate with data furnishers during disputes. It literally reduces your dispute to a checkbox. That's why online disputes have the lowest success rate.

### Why Certified Mail Is King

When you send a dispute via **USPS Certified Mail with Return Receipt Requested**, you create:

1. **Proof of mailing** — the post office stamps confirm you sent it
2. **Proof of delivery** — the green card comes back signed
3. **A legal paper trail** — if this ever goes to court (or to the CFPB), you have RECEIPTS
4. **Human review** — mail disputes are more likely to be reviewed by an actual person, not auto-processed through e-OSCAR

Cost: about $4-7 per letter. That's the price of a latte for something that could save you thousands in interest rates.

## The 609 Letter Myth — Let's Be Real With Each Other

You've seen the TikToks. "Send a Section 609 letter and DELETE everything off your credit report!"

Here's the truth, and I respect you too much to lie to you:

**Section 609 of the FCRA is about disclosure.** It gives you the right to REQUEST your file from the credit bureaus. That's it. It says the bureau must disclose to you what's in your file when you ask. It does NOT say they have to delete anything.

Section 609 says: *"Every consumer reporting agency shall, upon request... clearly and accurately disclose to the consumer all information in the consumer's file."*

That's a transparency provision. Not a deletion provision.

### Your REAL Weapon: Section 611

**Section 611 of the FCRA** — THIS is where the power is. Section 611 says:

*"If the completeness or accuracy of any item of information contained in a consumer's file is disputed by the consumer... the agency shall, free of charge, conduct a reasonable investigation to determine whether the disputed information is inaccurate."*

Key requirements under Section 611:

| Provision | What It Means For You |
|-----------|----------------------|
| **611(a)(1)** | Bureau MUST investigate your dispute |
| **611(a)(1)(A)** | They must complete the investigation within **30 days** (can extend to 45 if you provide additional info) |
| **611(a)(2)** | Bureau must forward ALL relevant information you provide to the data furnisher |
| **611(a)(5)** | If information is found to be inaccurate, incomplete, or unverifiable — it must be **DELETED or MODIFIED** |
| **611(a)(6)(B)(iii)** | After investigation, you can request the **METHOD OF VERIFICATION** — how did they verify the info? |
| **611(a)(7)** | The bureau must provide you results of the investigation within 5 business days of completion |

That last one — **611(a)(6)(B)(iii)** — is your secret weapon. When a dispute comes back "verified," you have the right to ask: "How did you verify this? Show me the method." If they can't produce it? That's a violation. More on this below.

## When Disputes Come Back "Verified" — Don't Panic, Escalate

So you sent your dispute letter. The bureau "investigated." And they sent you a letter saying the information was "verified as accurate."

This is NOT the end. This is round 2.

### Step 1: Request Method of Verification

Under **FCRA Section 611(a)(6)(B)(iii)**, you have the right to request the procedure used to determine the accuracy of the disputed information, including:
- The business name, address, and phone number of the furnisher they contacted
- The method used to verify the information

Send a letter requesting this information. If the bureau can't tell you HOW they verified it — or if the "method" was just the e-OSCAR auto-verify system — you have ammunition for your next step.

### Step 2: Dispute Directly with the Data Furnisher

Under **FCRA Section 623**, the original creditor or collector (the "data furnisher") has obligations too. They must:
- Conduct their OWN investigation when notified of a dispute
- Review ALL information provided by the consumer
- Report results back to the bureau
- Modify, delete, or permanently block reporting if information is found to be inaccurate

Send a dispute letter directly to the creditor/collector citing Section 623.

### Step 3: The CFPB Complaint — The Nuclear Option

The **Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)** is a federal agency that oversees credit bureaus. When you file a complaint through **consumerfinance.gov**, several things happen:

1. The complaint is forwarded to the bureau with a **federal tracking number**
2. The bureau MUST respond within **15 days** (acknowledging receipt) and resolve within **60 days**
3. CFPB complaints are taken FAR more seriously than regular disputes — they can lead to regulatory action against the bureau
4. Your complaint becomes part of a public database

**Important timing:** Dispute with the bureau first. Give them the legally required 30-45 days to investigate. THEN, if unresolved, file the CFPB complaint. This shows you followed proper procedure before escalating.

## Phase 1: The Foundation (Months 1–3)

This is where most people quit. It's not glamorous. You're not getting a black card. You're laying concrete. But without this phase, everything else crumbles.

### Move #1: Secured Credit Card

A secured card requires a deposit ($200–$500 typically) that becomes your credit limit. Yes, you're using your own money as collateral. I know it feels backwards. Do it anyway.

**The rules:**

- **Use 1–3% of your limit.** Got a $500 limit? Spend $5–$15 per month. That's it. A Netflix subscription. A Spotify payment. One coffee.
- **Pay in full BEFORE the statement closes.** Not before the due date — before the *statement closing date*. (More on this later — it's crucial.)
- **NEVER miss a payment.** Set up autopay for at least the minimum. Then also manually pay in full. Belt AND suspenders.

**Best first secured cards:** Discover it Secured (graduates to unsecured automatically), Capital One Platinum Secured (low deposit options), your credit union's secured card.

### Move #2: Credit Builder Loan

This is a backwards loan. Instead of getting money upfront, you make monthly payments into a locked savings account. Once you've paid the full amount, you get access to the money. The payments get reported to the bureaus as an installment loan.

Why this matters: it adds **account diversity**. You now have a revolving account (credit card) AND an installment account (credit builder loan). The scoring models love mix.

**Top picks:** Self (starts at $25/month), MoneyLion (competitive rates), your credit union's credit builder program.

### Move #3: Authorized User

If you know someone — parent, sibling, partner, trusted friend — who has a credit card that's:

- **Old** (5+ years is ideal)
- **Clean** (zero late payments ever)
- **Low utilization** (under 10% balance)

...ask them to add you as an authorized user. Their entire payment history on that card gets added to YOUR credit report. You don't even need to have the physical card. You don't need to spend a dollar. Their history becomes your history.

> **WARNING:** Make sure the card issuer reports authorized users to the bureaus. Most do (Amex, Chase, Discover, Capital One), but verify first. Also — if that person messes up their payments after adding you, that damage hits YOUR report too. Choose wisely.

### Move #4: Second Credit Card (Different Issuer)

Why different issuer? Two reasons:

1. **Different bureaus.** Some issuers report to all three bureaus, some don't. Having cards from multiple issuers increases the chance all three reports are being built simultaneously.
2. **Relationship diversification.** If one issuer decides to close your account (rare but possible), you still have history elsewhere.

If your first card was Discover, try Capital One or your credit union. If your first card was a credit union card, try a Discover or a fintech option.

## Utilization Management: The Number That Controls Your Score

Credit utilization is the second most important factor in your credit score (after payment history). It measures how much of your available credit you're using.

**The math:**
```
Utilization = (Total Balances) / (Total Credit Limits) x 100
```

**The targets:**

| Utilization % | Score Impact | The Vibe |
|---|---|---|
| 0% | Slightly negative (shows inactivity) | Ghost mode — bureaus wonder if you're alive |
| 1–3% | Maximum positive impact | Chef's kiss. The sweet spot. |
| 4–9% | Strong positive impact | Still excellent |
| 10–29% | Neutral to slightly positive | Acceptable but not optimized |
| 30–49% | Negative impact | Danger zone starts here |
| 50–74% | Significantly negative | Your score is hurting |
| 75–100% | Severely negative | Emergency. Pay this down immediately. |

**CRITICAL:** This applies to BOTH individual card utilization AND total utilization across all cards. If you have 3 cards with $1,000 limits each ($3,000 total), keep your total balance across all three under $300 (10%) and ideally under $90 (3%).

**Even more critical:** Keep individual card utilization low too. Having $0 on two cards and $900 on the third gives you 30% total utilization AND 90% on one card. Both hurt your score. Spread usage across cards if needed.

## The Balance Reporting Date Secret

This is the single most misunderstood concept in credit building. Read this twice.

Your credit card has TWO important dates:

1. **Statement closing date** — the date your billing cycle ends and your statement is generated
2. **Payment due date** — the date your payment is due (usually 21–25 days after the statement closes)

**Here's what most people don't know:** Your balance is reported to the credit bureaus on or near your **statement closing date**, NOT your payment due date.

So if your statement closes on the 15th and you pay your full balance on the 20th (before the due date on the 8th of next month) — congratulations, you paid on time. But the bureaus already recorded whatever balance you had on the 15th.

**The Crowned Consumer move:** Pay your balance down to 1–3% of your limit BEFORE your statement closing date. Then let the statement generate showing that tiny balance. Then pay the remaining balance before the due date.

This way, the bureaus see a low utilization ratio AND an on-time payment. Double win.

> **Pro tip:** Call your card issuer or check your app to find your exact statement closing date. Set a calendar reminder for 3 days before. Pay down your balance. Watch your score respond within 30 days.

- Guarantee outcomes

### When You NEED an Attorney

| Situation | Why AI Isn't Enough |
|---|---|
| You're being sued by a creditor or collector | You need someone who can appear in court and file legal motions |
| FCRA/FDCPA violations that could result in statutory damages | An attorney can calculate and pursue actual damages — potentially $1,000+ per violation |
| Identity theft that isn't being resolved through normal channels | Legal action may be needed to force bureau corrections |
| A creditor is willfully reporting inaccurate information after multiple disputes | This may warrant a lawsuit, and attorneys who handle FCRA cases often work on contingency (free to you — they get paid from the settlement) |
| You've been denied credit, housing, or employment due to reporting errors | Actual damages may be recoverable through legal action |

**Pro tip:** Many consumer protection attorneys offer FREE consultations. And FCRA/FDCPA attorneys often work on **contingency** — meaning they don't get paid unless YOU get paid. You literally have nothing to lose by consulting one.

Find consumer protection attorneys at:

## What Business Credit Actually Is

Business credit is credit attached to your **EIN** (Employer Identification Number) — NOT your Social Security Number.

Read that again.

When you build business credit correctly, you're creating an entirely separate credit profile for your business entity. That means:

- Your business can borrow money based on ITS creditworthiness
- Your personal credit doesn't take the hit if something goes sideways
- You can access WAY higher credit limits (we're talking $50K, $100K, $250K+ lines)
- Lenders evaluate your business's payment history, not yours

This is how the wealthy operate. They don't put everything on their personal Amex. They build entities, fund those entities with business credit, and keep their personal profiles clean as a whistle.

## The Foundation: 4 Things You Need Before Anything Else

Before you apply for a single business credit account, you need these four pillars in place:

### 1. LLC Formation
Register your LLC with your state. Cost varies ($50-$500 depending on your state). This gives your business a legal identity separate from you. You can do this through your Secretary of State website — skip the $800 "LLC formation services." It's a form. You can fill out a form.

### 2. EIN (Employer Identification Number)
This is your business's Social Security Number. Get it **free** from the IRS at irs.gov. Takes about 5 minutes online. Anyone who charges you for an EIN is scamming you. I said what I said.

### 3. Business Bank Account
Open a dedicated business checking account using your LLC docs and EIN. Do NOT mix personal and business funds. Ever. This is how you prove to lenders (and the IRS) that your business is real and operating. Most banks offer free business checking — Chase, Mercury, Bluevine, Relay.

### 4. Business Phone Number
Get a dedicated business phone number listed under your business name. Google Voice works for starting out. This matters because business credit bureaus verify your business exists by cross-referencing your phone listing. No listed number = "business not found" = application denied.

### The PAYDEX Score — Your Business Credit MVP

The D&B PAYDEX score is the big one. Here's how it breaks down:

- **80-100**: You pay on time or EARLY. This is where you want to be. Lenders love you.
- **50-79**: You're paying, but you're slow. Not a deal-breaker, but not impressive.
- **0-49**: You're paying late consistently. Red flags everywhere.

**Pro tip**: A PAYDEX of 80 means you pay exactly on time. Want higher? Pay your net-30 accounts in 15 days. Pay your net-15 accounts in 7 days. Speed is the game.
