Debt collectors are counting on one thing: **your ignorance.**

## Part 1: Know Your Enemy

### Who Is a "Debt Collector" Under the FDCPA?

The FDCPA applies to **third-party debt collectors** — meaning agencies hired to collect debts on behalf of original creditors, or companies that purchased your debt.

This includes:
- Collection agencies (Midland Credit, Portfolio Recovery, etc.)
- Attorneys who collect debts
- Debt buyers (companies that purchased your old debt for pennies on the dollar)

**Does NOT include:** The original creditor (Capital One, Chase, etc.) collecting their own debt — though some states have laws covering them.

## Part 2: What They Cannot Do — The FDCPA Violations List

Each of these is a **federal violation.** One violation = grounds for a lawsuit.

### Harassment and Abuse (§ 1692d)
- Cannot use **obscene or profane language**
- Cannot **threaten violence**
- Cannot **repeatedly call** with intent to harass (more than 1-2x/day is a red flag)
- Cannot **publish your name** as someone refusing to pay (debt shaming)

### False or Misleading Representations (§ 1692e)
- Cannot **falsely claim to be an attorney or government representative**
- Cannot **misrepresent the amount owed**
- Cannot **threaten legal action they cannot or will not take**
- Cannot **imply you committed a crime** for not paying
- Cannot **use a fake company name** to confuse you
- Cannot **threaten to seize property** they cannot legally seize

### Unfair Practices (§ 1692f)
- Cannot **collect fees or interest** not authorized by the original agreement or law
- Cannot **deposit a post-dated check early**
- Cannot **threaten to take or remove property** if they don't have the right to
- Cannot **contact you by postcard** (exposes your private info)

### Communication Rules (§ 1692c)
- Cannot call **before 8am or after 9pm**
- Cannot call your **workplace** if you've told them your employer doesn't permit it
- Must **stop contacting you** after a written cease-and-desist
- Cannot contact **third parties** (family, friends, employer) except to locate you

## Part 3: The Debt Validation Process — Your First Weapon

Before you sue, use this process. It often kills the debt entirely.

### Step 1: Send a Debt Validation Letter

Within **30 days** of the collector's first contact, send this letter via **certified mail with return receipt.**

---

**DEBT VALIDATION LETTER TEMPLATE**

[Your Name]
[Your Address]
[City, State, ZIP]
[Date]

[Debt Collector Name]
[Debt Collector Address]

**Re: Account #[Account Number if known] — REQUEST FOR DEBT VALIDATION**

To Whom It May Concern:

I am writing in response to your [letter/call] dated [date] regarding the above-referenced account. Under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA), 15 U.S.C. § 1692g, I hereby request that you provide me with the following validation of this alleged debt:

1. The name and address of the original creditor
2. Verification that the amount claimed is accurate and includes an itemization of all fees and interest
3. A copy of any agreement creating the alleged debt
4. Proof that your company is licensed to collect debts in [your state]
5. The date of the last payment on this account

Until this debt has been properly validated, please cease all collection activity, including any credit reporting.

This is not a refusal to pay. This is a formal request for validation as is my right under federal law.

Sincerely,
[Your Signature]
[Your Printed Name]

---

### What Happens Next

- **They validate:** Review the documents carefully. Check for errors in the amount, the creditor name, the account number. Any error = dispute ammunition.
- **They don't validate within 30 days:** They must cease collection activity. If they continue — that's a violation. Sue them.
- **They keep contacting you after a cease-and-desist:** Violation. Sue them.

If you want them to stop contacting you entirely (separate from validation), send this:

[Your Address]
[Date]

[Debt Collector Name]
[Address]

**Re: CEASE AND DESIST ALL COMMUNICATION**

Pursuant to my rights under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1692c(c), I hereby demand that you cease and desist all further communication with me regarding the alleged debt referenced above.

This includes but is not limited to phone calls, letters, emails, and any other form of communication.

## Part 5: How to Sue — The Federal Court Process

### Step 1: Document Everything

Before you file, build your case:
- Save every letter (photograph them, date-stamp)
- Keep a call log (date, time, what was said)
- Screenshot any voicemails
- Record calls where legal in your state (check your state's recording laws)

### Step 2: File a Complaint with the CFPB

Go to **consumerfinance.gov/complaint** — file a detailed complaint. This creates an official record and often triggers a response from the collector.

### Step 3: File in Federal District Court

You don't need a lawyer to file. Many people file **pro se** (representing themselves).

- Find your federal district court: **uscourts.gov**
- Filing fee: ~$402 (can be waived if income qualifies)
- You can recover: $1,000 statutory damages + actual damages + attorney fees if you hire one

### Step 4: Consider an FDCPA Attorney

Many FDCPA attorneys work **on contingency** — meaning you pay nothing. They get paid from the settlement. Search: "FDCPA attorney [your city]" or use **consumeradvocates.org** to find one.

## Part 6: Statute of Limitations — Time-Barred Debt

**Critical:** Collectors often try to collect debts that are **legally expired.**

Each state has a statute of limitations on debt (typically 3–6 years). Once expired:
- They **cannot sue you** for the debt
- If they threaten to sue — that's an FDCPA violation
- Paying even $1 on an expired debt can **restart the clock** — do NOT pay without knowing your state's SOL

**Check your state's statute of limitations at:** your state attorney general's website
