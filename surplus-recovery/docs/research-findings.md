# Research Findings

## How the Surplus Funds Recovery Industry Operates

### The Core Mechanism

When a property is sold at a foreclosure auction or tax sale, the sale price sometimes exceeds what was owed. This excess — called "surplus funds," "excess proceeds," or "overages" — is held by a government entity (county clerk, circuit court, county treasurer, or state comptroller) until claimed.

**Two primary types:**

1. **Foreclosure Surplus (Mortgage Foreclosure Excess Proceeds)**
   - Triggered when a mortgage foreclosure auction sale price exceeds the judgment amount + costs
   - Funds held by the court clerk (judicial foreclosure states) or trustee (non-judicial states)
   - Typical range: $2,000-$100,000+ per case
   - Rightful claimant: Former homeowner (usually), junior lienholders
   - Filing deadline: Varies by state — 60 days to several years

2. **Tax Sale Overages (Tax Deed/Tax Lien Excess Proceeds)**
   - Triggered when a tax sale auction price exceeds delinquent taxes + fees + penalties
   - Funds held by county treasurer, tax collector, or clerk
   - Typical range: $500-$50,000+ per case
   - Rightful claimant: Former property owner, mortgage holder, other lienholders
   - Filing deadline: Varies widely — some states as short as 1 year, others 5+ years

## Common Process Patterns

### Data Publication
- **Published lists:** Most counties publish surplus fund lists on their clerk/court/treasurer website
- **Formats:** PDF (most common), HTML tables, Excel/CSV (rare but ideal), portal search (requires case-by-case lookup)
- **Frequency:** After each sale, monthly, quarterly, or annually — varies by county
- **Completeness:** Often includes: case number, property address, sale amount, surplus amount, defendant/owner name. Sometimes missing key fields.

### Typical Operator Workflow
1. Pull surplus list from county
2. Filter for amounts worth pursuing (typically >$1,000-$2,000 minimum)
3. Identify the former owner/claimant from court or property records
4. Skip trace to find current contact information
5. Send outreach (typically direct mail first, then phone/email/SMS)
6. Follow up 5-10 times over 30-60 days
7. Explain the situation and the recovery service
8. Get signed assignment/agreement (contingency fee contract)
9. Gather required documents (ID, proof of ownership, notarized affidavit)
10. Prepare claim packet per county requirements
11. File the claim
12. Wait for county review and approval (30-180+ days)
13. Receive disbursement
14. Collect fee and send remainder to claimant

## Foreclosure Surplus vs Tax Overages — Key Differences

| Factor | Foreclosure Surplus | Tax Sale Overages |
|--------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Average amount | Higher ($5K-$50K+) | Lower ($1K-$15K) |
| Volume | Moderate | Higher in many counties |
| Claimant clarity | Usually former homeowner | Can be complex (multiple claimants) |
| Filing complexity | Moderate — court motion may be needed | Often simpler — administrative claim |
| Attorney required | Sometimes (varies by state) | Rarely |
| Competition | High in popular counties | Growing |
| Time to payout | 60-180 days | 30-120 days (often faster) |
| Data accessibility | Court records (often online) | Tax collector/treasurer sites |

**Recommendation:** Start with tax sale overages for faster wins and simpler filing, then add foreclosure surplus as operations mature.

## Key Filing Patterns by State

### Florida (Primary Target)
- **Foreclosure surplus:** Governed by Fla. Stat. §45.032-45.033
- **Filing:** Motion to court — may need attorney in some circuits
- **Tax deed surplus:** Fla. Stat. §197.582 — surplus held by clerk
- **Tax deed filing:** Written claim to clerk within prescribed period
- **Fee caps:** Florida does not have a specific statutory cap on recovery fees, but courts may scrutinize unreasonable fees
- **Statute of limitations:** Generally 1-2 years for tax deed surplus; foreclosure surplus varies
- **Data access:** County clerks publish lists; quality varies by county
- **Key counties:** Miami-Dade, Broward, Palm Beach, Orange, Hillsborough

### Texas (Secondary Target)
- **Tax sale surplus:** Tex. Tax Code §34.04 — excess proceeds returned to former owner
- **Filing:** Written request to taxing authority
- **Fee caps:** Texas enacted SB 1860 (2023) — caps recovery agent fees at 10% for tax sales and requires specific disclosures
- **CRITICAL:** Texas 10% fee cap makes this market less attractive for recovery agents
- **Statute of limitations:** 2 years for tax sale surplus
- **Data access:** County-by-county; some publish online, some require open records request

### Georgia (Tertiary Target)
- **Tax sale surplus:** O.C.G.A. §48-4-5 — excess funds held by tax commissioner
- **Filing:** Written claim to tax commissioner
- **Fee caps:** Georgia passed SB 292 (2023) — caps contingency fees and adds disclosure requirements
- **Statute of limitations:** 5 years
- **Data access:** Varies significantly by county

### Other Notable States
- **Ohio:** No fee cap (as of research date); county-by-county publication
- **North Carolina:** N.C.G.S. §105-374 — 5% fee cap on tax surplus (too low for operators)
- **Michigan:** Limited surplus rights after tax foreclosure
- **California:** Large amounts but complex process, lots of competition
- **New Jersey:** High surplus amounts; county surrogate/sheriff holds funds
- **Arizona:** Maricopa County publishes lists; relatively straightforward process
- **Pennsylvania:** County-by-county; some good opportunities

## Public Data Realities

### The Good
- Most counties have some online presence for surplus/overage lists
- Court records are generally public
- Property records are searchable
- Many counties publish after each sale

### The Bad
- **PDF hell:** Most lists are published as PDFs (scanned, sometimes handwritten)
- **Inconsistent schemas:** Every county uses different column names, formats, and structures
- **Missing data:** Claimant names are sometimes incomplete or use legal entity names
- **Stale data:** Some lists are updated infrequently
- **Anti-bot:** Some county sites have CAPTCHAs, IP blocking, or terms against scraping

### The Ugly
- **No standard API:** Zero counties provide structured APIs
- **Site redesigns:** County websites change without warning
- **Portal-only data:** Some counties require manual case-by-case searches (no bulk download)
- **Accuracy:** Surplus amounts on lists may not reflect final amounts after adjustments

## Operator Patterns Observed

### What Successful Operators Do
- Focus on 3-10 counties and go deep
- Send physical mail first (highest trust signal)
- Follow up relentlessly (7-12 touches)
- Build county-specific knowledge and templates
- Use VAs or contractors for data processing
- Partner with attorneys for complex cases
- Target $5,000+ surplus amounts (ignore small amounts)
- Move fast — contact claimants within days of list publication

### What Unsuccessful Operators Do
- Spray across too many counties/states without depth
- Rely only on email/SMS (low trust, high spam perception)
- Give up after 1-2 contact attempts
- Don't understand county-specific filing requirements
- Underestimate the paperwork and follow-through
- Over-invest in automation before validating the process manually

## Legal / Compliance Watchouts

1. **State fee caps:** Texas (10%), North Carolina (5%), Georgia (capped) — verify before entering any state
2. **Required disclosures:** Many states require specific language in recovery agreements
3. **CROA compliance:** If the service could be construed as "credit repair," additional regulations apply (unlikely for surplus recovery but worth noting)
4. **TCPA compliance:** SMS/phone outreach must comply with federal and state telecom regulations
5. **State-specific solicitation rules:** Some states regulate how recovery agents can contact claimants
6. **Unauthorized practice of law:** Preparing court motions or providing legal advice could cross this line
7. **Power of Attorney:** Some counties require specific POA forms rather than standard assignment agreements
8. **Notarization requirements:** Most claims require notarized affidavits or assignments

---

## What Seems to Work

1. **Direct mail** is still king for first contact — it's tangible, credible, and not immediately dismissed as spam
2. **County-specific expertise** beats generalist approaches
3. **Speed to contact** after list publication is a significant competitive advantage
4. **Professional presentation** (letterhead, website, verifiable business) dramatically improves conversion
5. **Phone follow-up** after mail converts at 3-5x email alone
6. **Simple, clear messaging** — "The county is holding $X from the sale of your property at [address]" is more effective than complex pitches
7. **Contingency fee model** removes barrier — "you pay nothing unless we recover your money"

---

## What Seems Overhyped

1. **"Passive income" claims** — This business requires active pipeline management
2. **"AI does all the work"** — AI helps with data processing and outreach but can't build trust or file complex claims
3. **"Just buy a course and you're ready"** — Courses teach the basics but county-specific knowledge comes from doing
4. **"100K/year with no experience"** — Possible but takes 6-12 months to build pipeline to that level
5. **"Any state works"** — Fee caps and filing complexity make some states non-viable
6. **"Automated calling converts"** — AI voice calls for this use case have low trust; real conversations convert better
7. **"You don't need an attorney"** — True for many cases, but complex ones (probate, multiple claimants, large amounts) benefit from attorney involvement
